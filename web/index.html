Root T

An option can be priced with a formula, or by simulating thousands of possible futures and averaging what it pays. These five chapters build the second way from a coin flip upwards, then test it against the first.

Random walks

Flip a coin. Heads, step up one; tails, step down one. Each step has variance 1, and the variances of independent steps add, so after n steps:

The walkers spread out, but slowly. Four times as many steps only doubles the spread.

This is where the name comes from. Everything that follows, from stock prices to simulation error, scales with a square root.

2

Stock prices

Prices move in percentages, not points, so the random walk goes in the exponent. Add up a year of small daily returns and the central limit theorem turns them into one normal draw Z:

The term is the Itô correction. Without it the average simulated price runs too high, because a +10% day followed by a −10% day leaves you at 99, not 100.

Switch the correction off and raise the volatility. The orange dot drifts above the dashed line, by a factor of eσ²T/2.

Averaging payoffs

A call option lets you buy at the strike K. If the stock ends above K you gain the difference; otherwise you walk away with nothing. Simulate many futures, record what the call pays in each, average, and discount back to today:

The shaded band is the 95% confidence interval the simulation reports about itself. It narrows like : a hundred times more paths for ten times more accuracy.

Here S₀ = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20% and T = 1 year. Push the strike up and watch how many paths pay nothing.

4

Convergence

One run proves little: its error is mostly luck. So price the call at seven path counts from 1,000 to 1,000,000, twenty times each with a different seed, and plot the error on log-log axes.

If theory holds, the standard error falls on a line of slope −½, and about 95% of runs catch the true price inside their interval. In the paper this came out at −0.499 and 92.9%.

Errors here are relative to the price. Try a strike of 140: the option is cheap, few paths pay, and the same number of paths buys far less accuracy.

The full surface

Now price every strike from 60 to 140 and every maturity from 0.1 to 2 years at once: 800 options, each checked against the exact formula.

Show the difference and switch between 10,000 and 1,000,000 paths. The vertical axis stays fixed, so the noise visibly flattens by about √100 = 10 times.

Two tricks make this fast enough to run live. The same random draws are reused for every σ and T, so the slider morphs the surface instead of re-rolling it. And because ST rises with Z, sorting Z once sorts every future, so each of the 800 prices costs a binary search rather than a pass over a million paths.
